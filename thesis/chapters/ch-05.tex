\chapter{Results} \label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the experiments described in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodolgy} and \ref{ch:implementation}.
Serialisation efficiency is presented first, followed by analysis cohorts and experiment coverage, direct
context inference, text-to-SQL, and a comparison of the two pipelines. The results cover operational
efficiency and retrieval accuracy as described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-metrics-implementation}.
Estimates retain equal weight per run within each reported analysis group. Intervals are pointwise
\qty{95}{\percent} database-bootstrap intervals, as presented in Section~\ref{sec:confidence-intervals}
(table cells show these intervals in brackets).

\section{Serialization efficiency} \label{sec:serialization-results}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/serialization_efficiency}

To measure the serialisation efficiency of each format, we tokenized the serialised database contents without
questions or additional prompt context. This allows us to compare the number of tokens needed to represent
the same data in different formats. We used the full Spider databases and the sampled BIRD databases
described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}, with one representative tokenizer from each of the Qwen~3.5,
Ministral~3, and Gemma~4 families. This gave us \num{558} measurements across \num{31} databases, six
formats, and three tokenizer families.

Table~\ref{tab:serialization-efficiency} shows the mean token counts across databases and tokenizer families,
with \num{60} measurements per format for Spider and \num{33} for BIRD. The ratios compare each format's mean
token count with the CSV mean for the same dataset. CSV required the fewest tokens on average in both
datasets. TOON was close to CSV, using \num{1.04} times as many tokens on Spider and \num{1.03} times as many
on BIRD. XML required the most tokens, with means \num{3.24} and \num{2.40} times those of CSV, respectively.
Markdown, YAML, and JSON fell between these formats.

These averages are strongly influenced by large databases. For example, \texttt{wta\_1} accounted for
\qty{98.76}{\percent} of all Spider CSV tokens counted across the three tokenizer families. The Spider mean
therefore does not describe the token count of a typical database.

\begin{figure}[htb]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/tokenizer_family_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{Median percentage increase in token count relative to the smallest count among the three
  tokenizer families for each database and format.}   \label{fig:tokenizer-family-comparison}
\end{figure}

The choice of tokenizer also influenced token counts, but the differences depended on the format.
Figure~\ref{fig:tokenizer-family-comparison} compares each tokenizer with the one that used the fewest tokens
for the same database and format, then reports the median percentage difference across databases. A median of
zero does not mean that the tokenizer used the fewest tokens for every database.

Qwen~3.5 had the lowest median increase for CSV and YAML on both datasets, while Ministral~3 had a median
increase of zero for Markdown and JSON. For TOON, Gemma~4 had a median increase of zero on Spider, whereas
Qwen~3.5 had the lowest median increase on BIRD. For XML, Ministral~3 had the lowest median increase on
Spider, while both Ministral~3 and Gemma~4 had small median increases on BIRD. These results show that no
tokenizer family was the most efficient across all formats.

Format choice also affected how many serialised databases fit within a limit of \num{131072} tokens. CSV and
TOON fit \num{19} of the \num{20} Spider databases with each tokeniser family, while the other formats fit
\num{18}. On BIRD, CSV and TOON fit \num{6} of the \num{11} sampled databases, compared with only \num{2} for
XML. Markdown fit \num{5} with Qwen~3.5 and \num{6} with Ministral~3 and Gemma~4. JSON fit \num{3}, \num{4},
and \num{3}, respectively. However, fitting the serialised data within this limit does not mean that a
complete request will fit. Instructions, the question, and space for generated output also need to be
considered. The following section therefore examines the coverage of the actual experimental runs.

\section{Analysis cohorts and experiment coverage} \label{sec:analysis-cohorts}

Before presenting the results, we need to distinguish between questions that have a non-empty reference
answer and questions that fit within the models' context limits. We therefore define two groups, called the
answerable cohort and the context-fit cohort. Table~\ref{tab:analysis-cohorts} shows the number of questions
and databases in each group.

The answerable cohort contains questions whose reference SQL query executed without error and returned at
least one row on the experimental database. This definition only establishes that a non-empty reference
result is available. It does not guarantee that the question is well formulated or that a model can answer it correctly.

The context-fit cohort contains questions with recorded evidence of fitting within the context limits across
all ten retained models and all seven configurations: six direct context inference formats and text-to-SQL.
Each question therefore requires a complete set of \num{70} run records. For direct context inference, the
initial check counted the fixed system prompt, including the serialised data, and reserved space for
generated output. Inclusion did not depend on whether the reference result was empty, whether the model
produced an answer, or whether the generated SQL executed successfully.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/analysis_cohorts}

Checking the reference queries revealed \num{49} empty results among \num{1034} Spider questions and
\num{426} among \num{1534} BIRD questions. The latter represent \qty{27.77}{\percent} of the BIRD questions
and were evaluated on the sampled databases described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. These counts
document the empty reference results following BIRD sampling. We found no reference SQL errors, missing
reference results, or inconsistencies in the recorded row counts.

The two cohorts overlap, but they do not contain exactly the same questions. They shared \num{807} Spider
questions and \num{244} BIRD questions. The context-fit cohort also contained \num{45} Spider questions and
\num{59} BIRD questions with empty reference results. Every question could be assigned or excluded using the
recorded criteria, and no required run records were missing. For BIRD, the context-fit cohort covered only
\texttt{student\_club} and \texttt{toxicology}, so any results based on this cohort describe only those two
sampled databases.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/experiment_outcomes}

We excluded the Qwen~3.5 27B runs because opening and closing reasoning tags were inconsistently generated,
preventing reliable separation of the reasoning trace from the final answer.
Table~\ref{tab:experiment-outcomes} summarises the outcomes for the remaining ten models across the full set
of experiments. Each run corresponds to one model, question, and retrieval configuration. Unlike the cohort
counts, these totals include all experimental questions. A successful run means that the pipeline completed,
not necessarily that its answer agreed with the reference.

Of the \num{179760} retained runs, \num{110185} completed and \num{69575} failed. Exceeding the context
window was the main cause of failure, accounting for \num{67536} runs, or \qty{97.1}{\percent} of all
failures. A further \num{1984} runs failed because SQL execution errors remained after the retry limit, while
\num{55} failed because no SQL statement could be extracted from the model output. These totals describe the
full set of experiments; the following sections specify which cohort is used for each comparison.

\section{Direct context inference} \label{sec:direct-results}

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/direct_operational_efficiency}

To compare operational efficiency across formats, we used the context-fit cohort defined in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis-cohorts}. Each format was evaluated on the same questions across all ten retained
models, including the recorded resource use of unsuccessful runs.
Table~\ref{tab:direct-operational-efficiency} shows the mean input and output token counts, Time to First
Token (TTFT), generation time after the first token, and total latency. Unlike the serialisation counts in
Section~\ref{sec:serialization-results}, the input counts here include the complete prompt rather than only
the serialised database contents.

CSV required the fewest input tokens on average, with \num{5366} per run on Spider and \num{31756} on BIRD.
XML required the most, with \num{20186} and \num{97190}, respectively. For the same question--model pairs,
the XML-to-CSV ratios of mean input tokens were \num{3.76} (\numrange{2.84}{4.38}) on Spider and \num{3.06}
(\numrange{2.94}{3.20}) on BIRD. TOON was close to CSV, with ratios of \num{1.07} (\numrange{1.04}{1.12}) and
\num{1.03} (\numrange{1.02}{1.04}). Markdown, YAML, and JSON fell between TOON and XML in mean input token use.

Output token counts varied much less across formats. The means spanned \numrange{81.2}{119.6} tokens on
Spider and \numrange{219.6}{260.5} on BIRD, including both reasoning traces and final answers. Formats that
required more input tokens did not always produce more output tokens. For example, YAML had the highest mean
output count on both datasets, even though JSON and XML required more input tokens.

The differences in input token use were accompanied by differences in response time. CSV had the lowest mean
total latency, at \qty{1.25}{\second} on Spider and \qty{8.02}{\second} on BIRD, while XML had the highest,
at \qty{7.42}{\second} and \qty{50.88}{\second}. The paired XML-to-CSV ratios were \num{5.91}
(\numrange{2.24}{8.31}) and \num{6.34} (\numrange{6.22}{6.47}), respectively. TOON was again close to CSV,
with latency ratios of \num{1.09} (\numrange{1.04}{1.16}) on Spider and \num{1.031} (\numrange{1.028}{1.033}) on BIRD.

Most of the latency difference between CSV and XML occurred before the first output token. Mean TTFT
increased from \qty{0.78}{\second} for CSV to \qty{6.45}{\second} for XML on Spider, and from
\qty{6.32}{\second} to \qty{46.87}{\second} on BIRD. Generation after the first token varied less, ranging
from \qty{0.48}{\second} to \qty{0.97}{\second} across formats on Spider and \qtyrange{1.70}{4.00}{\second}
on BIRD. These results show that compact formats were associated with lower token use and shorter response
times on the shared question set. However, the BIRD results cover only the two sampled databases in its
context-fit cohort, so the narrow intervals do not imply the same precision across the full BIRD dataset.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

To compare retrieval accuracy, we used the answerable cohort defined in Section~\ref{sec:analysis-cohorts},
containing \num{985} Spider questions and \num{1108} sampled BIRD questions. Each format was evaluated across
all ten retained models, giving \num{9850} Spider runs and \num{11080} BIRD runs per format. Runs that
exceeded the context limit or failed to complete were retained with zero scores.
Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-format} shows the mean NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L scores, which measure
different aspects of agreement with the reference answer as described in Section~\ref{sec:reference-answer-metrics}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_format.pdf}   \caption{Mean
  direct-context NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L by format on answerable questions.}
  \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-format}
\end{figure}

On Spider, JSON had the highest mean NEM score at \num{0.169}, followed by TOON at \num{0.166} and CSV at
\num{0.163}. These differences were small. For the same question--model pairs, JSON exceeded CSV by
\num{0.57} percentage points, but the interval (\numrange{-1.21}{2.34}) included zero, so this comparison
does not establish a clear advantage for JSON. CSV had the highest mean BERTScore and ROUGE-L scores, at
\num{0.812} and \num{0.336}, respectively. Its BERTScore exceeded XML's by \num{0.099}
(\numrange{0.003}{0.269}), while the difference between TOON and CSV was only \num{-0.0004}
(\numrange{-0.0020}{0.0011}). These comparisons show similar BERTScore results for CSV and TOON, but a
clearer difference between CSV and XML.

On BIRD, CSV had the highest mean scores on all three measures: NEM was \num{0.054}, BERTScore was
\num{0.463}, and ROUGE-L was \num{0.079}, with TOON close behind. XML had the lowest means, at \num{0.026},
\num{0.185}, and \num{0.042}, respectively. XML's NEM score was \num{2.75} percentage points below CSV's,
with a paired XML-minus-CSV difference of \num{-2.75} (\numrange{-5.12}{-0.82}). TOON was only \num{0.31}
percentage points below CSV, and its difference of \num{-0.31} (\numrange{-0.79}{0.14}) did not establish a
clear separation between the two formats. Because failed runs retain zero scores, these results reflect both
agreement with the reference answer and the ability to process the request within the context limit. They do
not measure accuracy only among runs that completed successfully.

\begin{figure}[htbp]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_model_size.pdf}
  \caption{Mean direct-context retrieval accuracy by model size and family on answerable questions,
  averaged over all six formats. Gemma~4 26B-A4B is positioned at its total nominal parameter count.}
  \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-model-size}
\end{figure}

We also compared model sizes within each family, averaging scores across all six formats
(Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-model-size}). Larger variants generally had higher mean scores, but the
differences varied between model families and datasets. On Spider, Gemma~4 26B-A4B exceeded E2B by
\num{15.52} NEM percentage points (\numrange{10.19}{21.78}), while Qwen~3.5 9B exceeded 0.8B by \num{14.45}
percentage points (\numrange{9.60}{20.16}). On BIRD, these gains were smaller: \num{1.58} percentage points
(\numrange{-0.02}{3.29}) for Gemma and \num{4.18} (\numrange{1.48}{7.02}) for Qwen. The Gemma interval
included zero, so the BIRD comparison does not establish a clear advantage for its larger variant.

Ministral~3 14B exceeded 8B by \num{2.01} NEM percentage points on Spider (\numrange{0.90}{3.21}), but by
only \num{0.09} on BIRD (\numrange{-0.72}{1.12}). Thus, a larger model did not consistently provide a clear
improvement across both datasets. These comparisons describe the evaluated variants within each family; they
do not isolate parameter count from other differences between the models.

\subsection{Database-size associations}

To examine the relationship between database size and retrieval accuracy, we compared the mean scores for
each database with the number of tokens needed to represent its contents. We used the answerable cohort,
which included all \num{20} Spider databases and all \num{11} sampled BIRD databases. For each format,
database size was measured as the mean token count across the three tokenizer families, while scores were
averaged across the retained question--model runs for that database.
Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-database-size-nem} shows this comparison for NEM, with database size on a
logarithmic scale.

\begin{figure}[htbp]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_database_size_nem.pdf}
  \caption{Mean direct-context NEM by serialised database size and format on answerable questions.     Each
    point represents one database, with point area indicating the number of questions. Size is the mean token
    count across the three tokenizer families, shown on a logarithmic scale. Trends and Pearson correlations
    are calculated separately for each dataset. A correlation interval is omitted when more than
  \qty{5}{\percent} of bootstrap samples have constant scores.}   \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-database-size-nem}
\end{figure}

Larger serialised databases generally had lower mean scores across all formats and all three metrics. For
CSV, the Pearson correlation between NEM and the logarithm of database token count was \num{-0.63} on Spider
(\numrange{-0.84}{-0.13}) and \num{-0.77} on BIRD (\numrange{-0.93}{-0.64}). Both intervals were below zero,
supporting a negative association in these comparisons. The figure shows the NEM results for the other formats.

BERTScore and ROUGE-L showed the same general pattern. Across formats, BERTScore correlations spanned
\numrange{-0.86}{-0.85} on Spider and \numrange{-0.84}{-0.60} on BIRD. ROUGE-L correlations ranged from
\numrange{-0.83}{-0.78} on Spider and \numrange{-0.82}{-0.60} on BIRD. For CSV, the BERTScore correlation
intervals were \numrange{-0.97}{-0.63} on Spider and \numrange{-0.95}{-0.71} on BIRD. The corresponding
ROUGE-L intervals were \numrange{-0.94}{-0.52} and \numrange{-0.96}{-0.71}, respectively.

These results include zero scores from runs that exceeded the context limit. All direct context inference
runs for Spider's \texttt{wta\_1} and five BIRD databases exceeded this limit in every format. The negative
associations therefore reflect both answer agreement and the inability to process some larger databases,
rather than accuracy only among completed runs. For XML on BIRD, only two databases had positive scores. Too
many bootstrap samples had constant scores to report a correlation interval under the criterion stated in the
figure caption.

Database size also varied alongside the domain, schema, and questions. We therefore cannot conclude from
these comparisons that size alone caused the lower scores. They describe the relationship between serialised
database size and retrieval accuracy in the evaluated databases.

\section{Text-to-SQL} \label{sec:text-to-sql-results}

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

We measured the tokens and time required per text-to-SQL run on the answerable cohort.
Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-operational-efficiency} shows the mean input and output token counts, Time to
First Token (TTFT), generation time after the first token, SQL execution time, total latency, and retries.
For each run, we added the tokens and times across the initial attempt and any correction attempts, including
those in runs that ultimately failed. Output counts include reasoning traces, and total latency includes SQL
execution time. These measurements therefore describe the cost of the complete attempt sequence rather than a
single model response.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_operational_efficiency}

BIRD runs required more tokens and took longer on average than Spider runs. Mean input and output counts were
\num{1873} and \num{211.1} tokens on BIRD, compared with \num{811} and \num{107.3} on Spider. Mean total
latency was \qty{1.033}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.862}{1.219}{\second}) on BIRD and \qty{0.536}{\second}
(\qtyrange{0.385}{0.667}{\second}) on Spider.

Generation after the first token accounted for most of the total latency on both datasets. It took
\qty{0.441}{\second} per run on Spider and \qty{0.849}{\second} on BIRD, compared with total first-token
waits of \qty{0.084}{\second} and \qty{0.176}{\second}, respectively. SQL execution contributed a much
smaller amount: \qty{0.010}{\second} on Spider and \qty{0.008}{\second} on BIRD. Mean retries per run were
also higher on BIRD, at \num{0.399}, compared with \num{0.173} on Spider.

The smallest model did not necessarily require the fewest tokens across the complete run. When Qwen~3.5 2B
was compared with 0.8B on the same questions, the 2B-to-0.8B ratios of mean input tokens were \num{0.589}
(\numrange{0.490}{0.699}) on Spider and \num{0.556} (\numrange{0.484}{0.621}) on BIRD. The corresponding
output-token ratios were \num{0.379} (\numrange{0.184}{0.614}) and \num{0.293} (\numrange{0.207}{0.400}). All
four intervals were below one, showing that 2B required fewer input and output tokens on average than 0.8B on
both datasets, including repeated prompts and correction outputs.

Qwen~3.5 2B also had lower mean total latency than 0.8B. The 0.8B model averaged \qty{1.662}{\second} and
\num{0.990} retries per run on Spider, and \qty{3.754}{\second} and \num{1.819} retries on BIRD. On the same
questions, the 2B-to-0.8B ratios of mean total latency were \num{0.432} (\numrange{0.207}{0.702}) on Spider
and \num{0.333} (\numrange{0.233}{0.457}) on BIRD. Both intervals were below one. Lower latency therefore
coincided with lower token use and fewer retries in this comparison; it does not show that a single
generation attempt was faster for the larger model.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

We evaluated text-to-SQL answers by comparing the textual query results with the reference answers using NEM,
BERTScore, and ROUGE-L. Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-retrieval-accuracy} shows the mean scores on the
answerable cohort alongside the proportion of runs whose generated SQL executed successfully within the retry
limit. These measurements distinguish agreement with the reference answer from the ability to execute a query.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_retrieval_accuracy}

Spider had higher mean scores than BIRD on all three measures. NEM was \num{0.634}, BERTScore was
\num{0.916}, and ROUGE-L was \num{0.740} on Spider, compared with \num{0.501}, \num{0.848}, and \num{0.517}
on BIRD, respectively. The Executed column reports final execution-success rates of \qty{95.23}{\percent} on
Spider and \qty{89.75}{\percent} on BIRD. On both datasets, successful execution was more common than
normalised exact agreement with the reference. A query that executes without error can still return a
different result, while differences in the textual representation can also prevent an exact match. Execution
success therefore does not establish answer correctness.

We also compared model sizes within each family. Figure~\ref{fig:text-to-sql-accuracy-by-model-size} shows
the mean scores for each model on the same answerable questions, including zero scores for failed runs.

\begin{figure}[htbp]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/text_to_sql_accuracy_by_model_size.pdf}
  \caption{Mean text-to-SQL NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L by model size and family on the answerable cohort.}
  \label{fig:text-to-sql-accuracy-by-model-size}
\end{figure}

Within each family, larger variants had higher observed mean scores on all three measures, but the gains
varied. On the same questions, Qwen~3.5 9B exceeded 0.8B by \num{44.06} NEM percentage points on Spider
(\numrange{39.24}{48.61}) and \num{46.03} on BIRD (\numrange{39.80}{52.86}), with most of the observed
increase occurring by 4B. Gemma~4 26B-A4B had the highest observed means, with NEM of \num{0.774} on Spider
and \num{0.669} on BIRD. Its gains over E2B were \num{9.34} percentage points (\numrange{5.35}{13.59}) and
\num{12.27} (\numrange{6.84}{18.06}), respectively; this does not establish an advantage over every other
model. Ministral~3 14B exceeded 8B by only \num{3.05} NEM percentage points on Spider (\numrange{0.00}{6.35})
and \num{2.53} on BIRD (\numrange{0.54}{4.48}). Unlike the other comparisons, the Ministral Spider interval
reached zero and did not establish a clear improvement. These comparisons do not isolate parameter count from
other differences between the evaluated variants.

\subsection{Error correction performance}

We evaluated whether correction produced executable SQL after an initial failure and how much additional
model-generation time it required. We used the answerable cohort and the retry policy described in
Section~\ref{sec:minimalist-text-to-sql}. Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-error-correction} groups runs by the
attempt on which SQL first executed successfully and shows how many still failed after all three retries. The
outcome percentages refer to all runs in the answerable cohort, while the recovery rate refers only to runs
whose initial attempt failed. Successful correction means that the query executed without error.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_error_correction}

Correction increased execution success on both datasets. On Spider, the success rate rose from
\qty{92.55}{\percent} on the first attempt to \qty{95.23}{\percent} after correction, a gain of \num{2.68}
percentage points (\numrange{1.66}{3.61}). On sampled BIRD, it rose from \qty{82.23}{\percent} to
\qty{89.75}{\percent}, a gain of \num{7.52} percentage points (\numrange{6.36}{8.76}). These comparisons used
the same \num{9850} Spider and \num{11080} BIRD runs before and after correction. Both intervals were above
zero, supporting an increase in execution success over the initial attempt.

Most initially failed runs still failed after correction, and most recoveries occurred on the first retry.
Retries recovered \num{264} of \num{734} initially failed Spider runs, or \qty{35.97}{\percent}
(\qtyrange{30.66}{41.49}{\percent}), and \num{833} of \num{1969} BIRD runs, or \qty{42.31}{\percent}
(\qtyrange{38.36}{47.37}{\percent}). The first retry accounted for \num{238} of these recoveries on Spider
and \num{680} on BIRD, with fewer additional recoveries from the remaining two retries.

All initially failed runs entered correction. Among these runs, including those that ultimately failed, the
mean number of retries was \num{2.32} on Spider and \num{2.24} on BIRD. The mean additional model-generation
time was \qty{2.38}{\second} (\qtyrange{1.53}{2.92}{\second}) on Spider and \qty{2.56}{\second}
(\qtyrange{2.21}{2.96}{\second}) on BIRD. This measurement includes first-token waits during correction. It
excludes SQL execution time because the stored records provide only aggregate database time, which cannot be
separated by attempt.

Most correction time was spent on runs that never produced executable SQL. These runs accounted for
\qty{95.3}{\percent} of all retry-generation time on Spider and \qty{88.3}{\percent} on BIRD. All \num{470}
Spider runs and \num{1136} BIRD runs that failed after correction used the three available retries, with mean
additional generation times of \qty{3.54}{\second} and \qty{3.92}{\second} per failed run, respectively.
Correction therefore recovered some executable queries, but most of its recorded generation time was spent on
unsuccessful attempts.

\section{Comparison of the two pipelines} \label{sec:common-cohort-comparison}

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

To compare retrieval accuracy between the two pipelines, we used the same \num{985} answerable Spider
questions and \num{1108} answerable sampled BIRD questions across all ten retained models. Each of the six
direct context inference formats was compared with text-to-SQL on the same question--model pairs. Runs that
exceeded the context limit or failed to complete were retained with zero scores.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-retrieval-comparison} shows the mean NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L scores and their
intervals for both pipelines.

\begin{figure}[htbp]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/pipeline_retrieval_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{Mean NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L for text-to-SQL (blue) and the six direct formats   (grey) on the
  answerable cohort, averaged over the ten retained models. }   \label{fig:pipeline-retrieval-comparison}
\end{figure}

On Spider, text-to-SQL had a mean NEM score of \num{0.634}, compared with \num{0.169} for JSON, the
highest-scoring direct format on this measure. The paired JSON-minus-SQL difference was \num{-0.465}
(\numrange{-0.507}{-0.422}). On sampled BIRD, text-to-SQL had a mean NEM score of \num{0.501}, compared with
\num{0.054} for CSV, the highest direct mean. The paired CSV-minus-SQL difference was \num{-0.447}
(\numrange{-0.524}{-0.380}).

CSV had the highest direct BERTScore and ROUGE-L means on both datasets, but text-to-SQL also had higher
scores in these comparisons. On Spider, the paired CSV-minus-SQL differences were \num{-0.104}
(\numrange{-0.229}{-0.038}) for BERTScore and \num{-0.403} (\numrange{-0.450}{-0.359}) for ROUGE-L. On
sampled BIRD, the corresponding differences were \num{-0.385} (\numrange{-0.661}{-0.137}) and \num{-0.438}
(\numrange{-0.508}{-0.367}).

Because failed runs retain zero scores, these comparisons reflect both agreement with the reference answer
and the ability of each pipeline to complete the request. They do not isolate answer agreement among
successful runs or measure SQL execution success. As with the earlier accuracy results, higher scores
indicate closer agreement with the textual reference rather than a direct verification of factual correctness.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy by database size} \label{sec:pipeline-accuracy-by-database-size}

We also compared retrieval accuracy within three database-size tiers: small, with at most \num{1500} tokens;
medium, with more than \num{1500} and at most \num{10000}; and large, with more than \num{10000}. Each
database was assigned one fixed tier using its mean payload token count across all six formats and three
tokenizer families from Section~\ref{sec:serialization-results}. Unlike the format-specific sizes in the
earlier direct-context analysis, this average keeps tier membership identical for every configuration in the
head-to-head comparison. It excludes questions and other prompt context.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/pipeline_size_cohorts}

and fifteen large databases, with \num{265}, \num{400}, and \num{1428} questions, respectively. Within each
tier, we retained the same question--model pairs across both pipelines, including zero scores for failed

Text-to-SQL had higher mean scores than every direct format on all three metrics in every tier
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-retrieval-by-database-size}). Every paired direct-minus-SQL interval was below
zero. SQL's mean NEM decreased from \num{0.723} in the small tier to \num{0.671} in the medium tier and
\num{0.504} in the large tier. The highest direct means were \num{0.239} and \num{0.210} for JSON in the
small and medium tiers, and \num{0.061} for TOON in the large tier. Their paired differences from SQL were
\num{-0.484} (\numrange{-0.541}{-0.442}), \num{-0.461} (\numrange{-0.539}{-0.373}), and \num{-0.443}
(\numrange{-0.503}{-0.387}), respectively. Thus, the observed NEM gap was smaller in the large tier, but both
pipelines also had lower mean NEM there.

The other metrics showed a different pattern in the size of the gap. The highest-scoring direct format's
BERTScore was about \numrange{0.053}{0.054} below SQL in the small and medium tiers, compared with
\num{0.345} in the large tier. The corresponding ROUGE-L gaps were \num{0.356}, \num{0.366}, and \num{0.445}.
These contrasts describe observed gaps rather than establishing differences between tiers, and the direct
format with the highest mean varied by metric and tier.

The tier results do not isolate database size from differences in workload composition, domain, schema, or
questions. Some large databases also exceeded the context limit in every direct format. The comparisons
therefore describe end-to-end reference-answer agreement, including context failures, rather than accuracy
only among completed runs or a causal effect of size.

\subsection{Operational efficiency} \label{sec:pipeline-operational-efficiency}

\begin{figure}[htb]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/common_cohort_token_usage.pdf}   \caption{Mean
    input and output tokens on the shared context-fit workload, including SQL failures and all correction
    attempts. Input tokens use a logarithmic scale; output tokens use a linear scale. Whiskers show pointwise
  \qty{95}{\percent} intervals.}   \label{fig:common-cohort-token-usage}
\end{figure}

We compared operational efficiency on the same context-fit questions used in the direct-format analysis:
\num{852} from \num{18} Spider databases and \num{303} from two sampled BIRD databases. Both pipelines used
identical question--model pairs, retaining the recorded costs of \num{372} unsuccessful SQL runs on Spider
and \num{231} on BIRD. SQL token counts and timings include all initial and correction attempts, with total
latency also including SQL execution.

Text-to-SQL used fewer input tokens than every direct format (Figure~\ref{fig:common-cohort-token-usage}).
CSV required \num{7.19} times as many input tokens (\numrange{2.38}{13.17}) on Spider and \num{28.91} times
as many (\numrange{24.71}{35.63}) on BIRD. Output differences were less consistent on Spider: the CSV-to-SQL
output-token ratio of \num{0.91} (\numrange{0.65}{1.12}) did not establish a clear difference, and only XML's
ratio interval excluded one. On BIRD, all direct formats produced more output tokens, with ratio intervals above one.

\begin{figure}[htbp]   \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/common_cohort_latency.pdf}   \caption{Mean
    latency components on the shared context-fit workload. SQL includes all initial and correction work,
  including unsuccessful runs. Whiskers show pointwise \qty{95}{\percent}   intervals for total latency.}
  \label{fig:common-cohort-latency}
\end{figure}

Text-to-SQL also had lower mean total latency than every direct format
(Figure~\ref{fig:common-cohort-latency}). On Spider, CSV averaged \qty{1.255}{\second}, compared with
\qty{0.466}{\second} for SQL. The CSV-to-SQL latency ratio was \num{2.69} (\numrange{1.06}{4.27}), while
XML's ratio was \num{15.93} (\numrange{2.60}{31.80}). On BIRD, the corresponding ratios were \num{10.43}
(\numrange{9.54}{11.51}) for CSV and \num{66.17} (\numrange{59.32}{74.40}) for XML.

Most of the latency difference occurred in TTFT. Generation-time differences were less consistent on Spider,
while all direct formats had longer mean generation times than SQL on BIRD. SQL execution contributed only
\qty{0.005}{\second} per run on Spider and \qty{0.007}{\second} on BIRD.

Lower mean latency did not mean faster responses in every case: SQL was faster than CSV in
\qty{58.9}{\percent} of Spider pairs (\qtyrange{43.9}{72.3}{\percent}) and \qty{98.78}{\percent} of BIRD
pairs (\qtyrange{98.73}{98.83}{\percent}). The small Spider tier, defined in
Section~\ref{sec:pipeline-accuracy-by-database-size}, contained \num{273} context-fit questions across eight
databases. CSV was faster in \qty{68.2}{\percent} of its question--model pairs
(\qtyrange{61.8}{75.2}{\percent}), averaging \qty{0.261}{\second} compared with \qty{0.298}{\second} for SQL.
However, its mean advantage of \qty{0.037}{\second} (\qtyrange{-0.030}{0.121}{\second}) remained uncertain.
SQL's lower overall mean was driven mainly by large-database pairs: they represented only
\qty{20.2}{\percent} of the Spider workload, but their larger latency differences in SQL's favour outweighed
CSV's smaller advantages elsewhere. The narrow BIRD interval reflects similar win rates in only two sampled
databases. Since accuracy and efficiency use different cohorts, these results do not establish a joint
cost--accuracy trade-off on the same question set.
