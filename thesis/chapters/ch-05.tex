\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the experiments described in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodolgy} and
\ref{ch:implementation}. Serialization efficiency is presented first, followed by analysis cohorts and
experiment coverage, Direct Context Inference, Text-to-SQL, and a comparison of the two pipelines.
The results operational efficiency and retrieval accuracy as described in \ref{sec:evaluation-metrics-implementation}.
Estimates retain equal weight per run within each dataset. Intervals are pointwise
\qty{95}{\percent} database-bootstrap intervals, as presented in Section~\ref{sec:confidence-intervals}
(table cells show these intervals in brackets).

\section{Serialization efficiency}
\label{sec:serialization-results}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/serialization_efficiency}

To measure the serialization efficiency of each format, we tokenized the serialized database contents
without questions or additional prompt context. This allows us to compare the number of tokens needed to
represent the same data in different formats. We used the full Spider databases and the sampled BIRD
databases described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}, with one representative tokenizer from each of
Qwen~3.5, Ministral~3, and Gemma~4 families. This gave us \num{558} measurements across \num{31}
databases, six formats, and three tokenizer families.

Table~\ref{tab:serialization-efficiency} shows the mean token counts across databases and tokenizer
families, with \num{60} measurements per format for Spider and \num{33} for BIRD. The ratios compare each
format's mean token count with the CSV mean for the same dataset. CSV required the fewest tokens on
average in both datasets. TOON was close to CSV, using \num{1.04} times as many tokens on Spider and
\num{1.03} times as many on BIRD. XML required the most tokens, with means \num{3.24} and \num{2.40}
times those of CSV, respectively. Markdown, YAML, and JSON fell between these formats.

These averages are strongly influenced by large databases. For example, \texttt{wta\_1} accounted for
\qty{98.76}{\percent} of all Spider CSV tokens counted across the three tokenizer families. The Spider
mean therefore does not describe the token count of a typical database.

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/tokenizer_family_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{Median percentage increase in token count relative to the smallest count among the three
    tokenizer families for each database and format.}
  \label{fig:tokenizer-family-comparison}
\end{figure}

The choice of tokenizer also influenced token counts, but the differences depended on the format.
Figure~\ref{fig:tokenizer-family-comparison} compares each tokenizer with the one that used the fewest
tokens for the same database and format, then reports the median percentage difference across databases.
A median of zero does not mean that the tokenizer used the fewest tokens for every database.

Qwen~3.5 had the lowest median increase for CSV and YAML on both datasets, while Ministral~3 had a median
increase of zero for Markdown and JSON. For TOON, Gemma~4 had a median increase of zero on Spider,
whereas Qwen~3.5 had the lowest median increase on BIRD. For XML, Ministral~3 had the lowest median
increase on Spider, while both Ministral~3 and Gemma~4 had small median increases on BIRD. These results
show that no tokenizer family was the most efficient across all formats.

Format choice also affected how many serialized databases fit within a limit of \num{131072} tokens.
CSV and TOON fit \num{19} of the \num{20} Spider databases with each tokenizer family, while the other
formats fit \num{18}. On BIRD, CSV and TOON fit \num{6} of the \num{11} sampled databases, compared
with only \num{2} for XML. Markdown fit \num{5} with Qwen~3.5 and \num{6} with Ministral~3 and Gemma~4.
JSON fit \num{3}, \num{4}, and \num{3}, respectively. However, fitting the serialized data within this
limit does not mean that a complete request will fit. Instructions, the question, and space for generated
output also need to be considered. The following section therefore examines the coverage of the actual
experimental runs.

\section{Analysis cohorts and experiment coverage}
\label{sec:analysis-cohorts}

Before presenting the results, we need to distinguish between questions that have a non-empty reference
answer and questions that fit within the models' context limits. We therefore define two groups, called
the answerable cohort and the context-fit cohort. Table~\ref{tab:analysis-cohorts} shows the number of
questions and databases in each group.

The answerable cohort contains questions whose reference SQL query executed without error and returned
at least one row on the experimental database. This definition only establishes that a non-empty
reference result is available. It does not guarantee that the question is well formulated or that a model
can answer it correctly.

The context-fit cohort contains questions with recorded evidence of fitting within the context limits
across all ten retained models and all seven configurations: six Direct Context Inference formats and
Text-to-SQL. Each question therefore requires a complete set of \num{70} run records. For Direct Context
Inference, the initial check counted the fixed system prompt, including the serialized data, and reserved
space for generated output. Inclusion did
not depend on whether the reference result was empty, whether the model produced an answer, or whether
the generated SQL executed successfully.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/analysis_cohorts}

Checking the reference queries revealed \num{49} empty results among \num{1034} Spider questions and
\num{426} among \num{1534} BIRD questions. The latter represent \qty{27.77}{\percent} of the BIRD
questions and were evaluated on the sampled databases described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}.
These counts document the empty reference results following BIRD sampling. We found no reference SQL
errors, missing reference results, or inconsistencies in the recorded row counts.

The two cohorts overlap, but they do not contain exactly the same questions. They shared \num{807}
Spider questions and \num{244} BIRD questions. The context-fit cohort also contained \num{45} Spider
questions and \num{59} BIRD questions with empty reference results. Every question could be assigned or
excluded using the recorded criteria, and no required run records were missing. For BIRD, the context-fit
cohort covered only \texttt{student\_club} and \texttt{toxicology}, so any results based on this cohort
describe only those two sampled databases.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/experiment_outcomes}

We excluded the Qwen~3.5 27B runs because the reasoning trace could not be reliably separated from the
final answer. Table~\ref{tab:experiment-outcomes} summarizes the outcomes for the remaining ten models
across the full set of experiments. Each run corresponds to one model, question, and retrieval
configuration. Unlike the cohort counts, these totals include all experimental questions. A successful
run means that the pipeline completed, not necessarily that its answer agreed with the reference.

Of the \num{179760} retained runs, \num{110185} completed and \num{69575} failed. Exceeding the context
window was the main cause of failure, accounting for \num{67536} runs, or \qty{97.1}{\percent} of all
failures. A further \num{1984} runs failed because SQL execution errors remained after the retry limit,
while \num{55} failed because no SQL statement could be extracted from the model output. These totals
describe the full set of experiments; the following sections specify which cohort is used for each
comparison.

\section{Direct Context Inference}
\label{sec:direct-results}

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/direct_operational_efficiency}

To compare operational efficiency across formats, we used the context-fit cohort defined in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis-cohorts}. Each format was evaluated on the same questions across all ten
retained models, including the recorded resource use of unsuccessful runs.
Table~\ref{tab:direct-operational-efficiency} shows the mean input and output token counts, Time to
First Token (TTFT), generation time after the first token, and total latency. Unlike the serialization
counts in Section~\ref{sec:serialization-results}, the input counts here include the complete prompt
rather than only the serialized database contents.

CSV required the fewest input tokens on average, with \num{5366} per run on Spider and \num{31756} on
BIRD. XML required the most, with \num{20186} and \num{97190}, respectively. Comparing the same
question--model pairs, the XML-to-CSV ratios of mean input tokens were \num{3.76}
(\numrange{2.84}{4.38}) on Spider and \num{3.06} (\numrange{2.94}{3.20}) on BIRD. TOON was close to
CSV, with ratios of \num{1.07} (\numrange{1.04}{1.12}) and \num{1.03} (\numrange{1.02}{1.04}).
Markdown, YAML, and JSON fell between TOON and XML in mean input token use.

Output token counts varied much less across formats. The means ranged from \numrange{81.2}{119.6}
tokens on Spider and \numrange{219.6}{260.5} on BIRD, including both reasoning traces and final
answers. Formats that required more input tokens did not always produce more output tokens. For
example, YAML had the highest mean output count on both datasets, even though JSON and XML required
more input tokens.

The differences in input token use were accompanied by differences in response time. CSV had the lowest
mean total latency, at \qty{1.25}{\second} on Spider and \qty{8.02}{\second} on BIRD, while XML had
the highest, at \qty{7.42}{\second} and \qty{50.88}{\second}. The paired XML-to-CSV ratios were
\num{5.91} (\numrange{2.24}{8.31}) and \num{6.34} (\numrange{6.22}{6.47}), respectively. TOON was
again close to CSV, with latency ratios of \num{1.09} (\numrange{1.04}{1.16}) on Spider and
\num{1.031} (\numrange{1.028}{1.033}) on BIRD.

Most of the latency difference between CSV and XML occurred before the first output token. Mean TTFT
increased from \qty{0.78}{\second} for CSV to \qty{6.45}{\second} for XML on Spider, and from
\qty{6.32}{\second} to \qty{46.87}{\second} on BIRD. Generation after the first token varied less,
ranging from \qtyrange{0.48}{0.97}{\second} across formats on Spider and
\qtyrange{1.70}{4.00}{\second} on BIRD. These results show that compact formats were associated with
lower token use and shorter response times on the shared question set. However, the BIRD results cover
only the two sampled databases in its context-fit cohort, so the narrow intervals do not imply the same
precision across the full BIRD dataset.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

Accuracy used the answerable cohort, including zero-scored context failures. Each format therefore
summarized \num{9850} Spider and \num{11080} BIRD model--question runs.
Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-format} reports all three retrieval metrics defined in
Section~\ref{sec:reference-answer-metrics}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_format.pdf}
  \caption{Mean direct-context NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L by format on answerable questions.
    Failed runs retain zero scores. Whiskers show pointwise \qty{95}{\percent} intervals.}
  \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-format}
\end{figure}

Spider's observed NEM means were close: JSON had the highest value (\num{0.169}), followed by TOON
(\num{0.166}) and CSV (\num{0.163}). JSON exceeded CSV by \num{0.57} percentage points, but the paired
interval (\numrange{-1.21}{2.34}) included zero. CSV had the highest BERTScore (\num{0.812}) and
ROUGE-L (\num{0.336}). Its BERTScore exceeded XML by \num{0.099} (\numrange{0.003}{0.269}), whereas
the TOON-minus-CSV difference was only \num{-0.0004} (\numrange{-0.0020}{0.0011}). Thus, the evidence
separated compact formats from XML more clearly in BERTScore than in NEM.

On BIRD, CSV had the highest means: NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L were \num{0.054}, \num{0.463}, and
\num{0.079}, with TOON close behind. XML had the lowest means (\num{0.026}, \num{0.185}, and
\num{0.042}). Its NEM difference from CSV was \num{-2.75} percentage points
(\numrange{-5.12}{-0.82}), while TOON's difference was \num{-0.31}
(\numrange{-0.79}{0.14}). These estimates include the formats' different context-overflow rates;
they do not isolate answer agreement conditional on inference taking place.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_model_size.pdf}
  \caption{Mean direct-context retrieval accuracy by model size and family on answerable questions,
    averaged over all six formats. Lines join models within a family; intervals are pointwise
    \qty{95}{\percent}. Gemma~4 26B-A4B is positioned at its total nominal parameter count.}
  \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-model-size}
\end{figure}

Larger variants generally had higher observed scores, but the size association varied by family and
dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-model-size}). On Spider, Gemma~4 26B-A4B exceeded E2B by
\num{15.52} NEM percentage points (\numrange{10.19}{21.78}); Qwen~3.5 9B exceeded 0.8B by
\num{14.45} (\numrange{9.60}{20.16}). On BIRD, the corresponding gains were \num{1.58}
(\numrange{-0.02}{3.29}) and \num{4.18} (\numrange{1.48}{7.02}). Ministral~3 14B's gain over 8B was
\num{2.01} points on Spider (\numrange{0.90}{3.21}) but only \num{0.09} on BIRD
(\numrange{-0.72}{1.12}). The latter comparisons do not establish a uniform benefit from nominal
parameter count.

\subsection{Database-size associations}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_database_size_nem.pdf}
  \caption{Direct-context NEM on answerable questions by database payload size and format. Points
    average the retained model--question runs for each database; area indicates question count.
    Size is the mean payload-token count across the three tokenizer families, on a logarithmic axis.
    Dataset-specific trends and Pearson correlations have pointwise \qty{95}{\percent} intervals.

  \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-database-size-nem}
\end{figure}

All \num{20} Spider and \num{11} sampled BIRD databases remained represented in the answerable cohort.
Database-level mean scores were negatively associated with log payload size across all formats and
metrics. For CSV, the NEM correlation was \num{-0.63} on Spider (\numrange{-0.84}{-0.13}) and
\num{-0.77} on BIRD (\numrange{-0.93}{-0.64}); Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-database-size-nem}
shows the other formats. The recalculated BERTScore correlations ranged from \numrange{-0.86}{-0.85}
on Spider and \numrange{-0.84}{-0.60} on BIRD; ROUGE-L correlations ranged from
\numrange{-0.83}{-0.78} and \numrange{-0.82}{-0.60}. For CSV, BERTScore intervals were
\numrange{-0.97}{-0.63} and \numrange{-0.95}{-0.71}. The corresponding ROUGE-L intervals were
\numrange{-0.94}{-0.52} and \numrange{-0.96}{-0.71}, respectively.

These associations include structural zero scores from context overflow. All direct runs for Spider's
\texttt{wta\_1} and five BIRD databases exceeded the context limit in every format. Only two BIRD
databases had positive XML scores, and the XML correlation interval was not estimable under the stated
bootstrap criterion. Database size also varied with domain, schema, and questions, so these results
describe associations rather than an isolated causal effect of size.

\section{Text-to-SQL}
\label{sec:text-to-sql-results}

All results in this section use the answerable cohort from Section~\ref{sec:analysis-cohorts}:
\num{985} Spider and \num{1108} sampled-BIRD questions, giving \num{9850} and \num{11080} model--question runs.
Failed runs retain their recorded operational costs and zero retrieval scores.

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-operational-efficiency} reports the five shared operational metrics, SQL execution
time, and retries. Input and output tokens, first-token waits, and generation after the first token are
accumulated across all attempts, including unsuccessful correction sequences. Output counts include reasoning
traces; total response latency also includes the recorded aggregate SQL execution time.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_operational_efficiency}

Mean input and output use was \num{811} and \num{107.3} tokens on Spider, compared with \num{1873} and
\num{211.1} on BIRD. Mean total latency was \qty{0.536}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.385}{0.667}{\second}) and
\qty{1.033}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.862}{1.219}{\second}), respectively. Generation after the first token
accounted for most of these totals: \qty{0.441}{\second} on Spider and \qty{0.849}{\second} on BIRD,
compared with cumulative TTFT of \qty{0.084}{\second} and \qty{0.176}{\second}. SQL execution contributed
only \qty{0.010}{\second} and \qty{0.008}{\second}; mean retries were \num{0.173} and \num{0.399}.

Accumulated token use varied substantially between models (Figure~\ref{fig:text-to-sql-token-usage}).
For Qwen~3.5 2B relative to 0.8B, paired mean input-token ratios were \num{0.589}
(\numrange{0.490}{0.699}) on Spider and \num{0.556} (\numrange{0.484}{0.621}) on BIRD.
Output-token ratios were smaller still: \num{0.379} (\numrange{0.184}{0.614}) and \num{0.293}
(\numrange{0.207}{0.400}). These are accumulated request costs, including repeated prompts and outputs.

The smallest model did not have the lowest accumulated latency (Figure~\ref{fig:text-to-sql-latency}).
Qwen~3.5 0.8B averaged \qty{1.662}{\second} and \num{0.990} retries on Spider, and \qty{3.754}{\second}
and \num{1.819} retries on BIRD. For 2B relative to 0.8B, paired mean-latency ratios were \num{0.432}
(\numrange{0.207}{0.702}) and \num{0.333} (\numrange{0.233}{0.457}), respectively.
Lower latency therefore coincided with lower token use and fewer retries in this comparison.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_retrieval_accuracy}

Spider had higher observed mean agreement on all three metrics: NEM was \num{0.634}, BERTScore \num{0.916},
and ROUGE-L \num{0.740}, compared with \num{0.501}, \num{0.848}, and \num{0.517} on BIRD
(Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-retrieval-accuracy}). The Executed column reports final execution success

The gap between execution success and NEM shows that an executable

(Figure~\ref{fig:text-to-sql-accuracy-by-model-size}). The largest NEM change was within Qwen~3.5:

These within-family comparisons describe the evaluated models rather than an isolated effect of parameter count.

\subsection{Error correction performance}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_error_correction}

Under the retry policy in Section~\ref{sec:minimalist-text-to-sql}, execution success increased from
\qty{92.55}{\percent} on the first attempt to \qty{95.23}{\percent} on Spider, and from
\qty{82.23}{\percent} to \qty{89.75}{\percent} on BIRD. Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-error-correction}
groups successful runs by the attempt on which SQL first executed successfully and lists terminal
failures; outcome percentages use all eligible runs. Retries recovered \num{264} of \num{734}
initially failed Spider runs, or \qty{35.97}{\percent} (\qtyrange{30.66}{41.49}{\percent}), and \num{833}
of \num{1969} BIRD runs, or \qty{42.31}{\percent} (\qtyrange{38.36}{47.37}{\percent}). Across all
\num{9850} and \num{11080} eligible runs, these recoveries were paired execution-success gains of
\num{2.68} (\numrange{1.66}{3.61}) and \num{7.52} (\numrange{6.36}{8.76}) percentage points.
They measure recovered execution, not recovered answer accuracy.

All initially failed runs entered correction. Mean retries were \num{0.173} per eligible Spider run and
\num{0.399} per eligible BIRD run; among runs entering correction, they were \num{2.32} and \num{2.24}.
Added model-generation time averaged \qty{0.177}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.078}{0.269}{\second}) per eligible
Spider run and \qty{0.455}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.337}{0.587}{\second}) per eligible BIRD run.
Using only runs entering correction as the denominator, the means were \qty{2.38}{\second}
(\qtyrange{1.53}{2.92}{\second}) and \qty{2.56}{\second} (\qtyrange{2.21}{2.96}{\second}).
This retry-generation overhead includes first-token waits. Retry-specific SQL execution time cannot be
isolated from the stored aggregate database time and is excluded from these added-time measurements.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/text_to_sql_error_correction.pdf}
  \caption{Additional Text-to-SQL model-generation time on the answerable cohort, decomposed by terminal
    outcome (left) and retry stage (right). Both panels report seconds per eligible run, using all
    \num{9850} Spider and \num{11080} sampled-BIRD runs as denominators. Time includes first-token waits
    in correction attempts and excludes SQL execution; terminal failures retain their recorded costs.}
  \label{fig:text-to-sql-error-correction}
\end{figure}

The first retry supplied most recoveries: \num{238} on Spider and \num{680} on BIRD. Later stages still
accounted for substantial generation time (Figure~\ref{fig:text-to-sql-error-correction}). The three stages
added \qty{0.063}{\second}, \qty{0.057}{\second}, and \qty{0.057}{\second} per eligible Spider run,
and \qty{0.174}{\second}, \qty{0.143}{\second}, and \qty{0.138}{\second} per eligible BIRD run.
Terminal failures accounted for \qty{95.3}{\percent} of all retry-generation time on Spider and
\qty{88.3}{\percent} on BIRD. The \num{470} and \num{1136} failed sequences each used all three retries,
with mean added generation time of \qty{3.54}{\second} and \qty{3.92}{\second} per failed run.
Thus, most recorded correction cost belonged to sequences that never produced executable SQL.

\section{Comparison of the two pipelines}
\label{sec:common-cohort-comparison}

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

The accuracy comparison used all \num{985} answerable Spider questions and \num{1108} answerable BIRD
questions, paired across the ten retained models and all six direct formats. Context overflows and other
terminal failures remained in the comparison with zero scores. Text-to-SQL had higher mean NEM,
BERTScore, and ROUGE-L than every direct format on both datasets; all paired difference intervals
excluded zero (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-retrieval-comparison}).

On Spider, Text-to-SQL reached NEM \num{0.634}, compared with \num{0.169} for JSON, the highest direct
mean. The paired JSON-minus-SQL difference was \num{-0.465} (\numrange{-0.507}{-0.422}). On sampled
BIRD, Text-to-SQL reached \num{0.501}, compared with CSV's \num{0.054}, a difference of
\num{-0.447} (\numrange{-0.524}{-0.380}). CSV had the highest direct BERTScore and ROUGE-L means
on both datasets, but its paired differences from SQL were still \num{-0.104}
(\numrange{-0.229}{-0.038}) and \num{-0.403} (\numrange{-0.450}{-0.359}) on Spider, and
\num{-0.385} (\numrange{-0.661}{-0.137}) and \num{-0.438} (\numrange{-0.508}{-0.367}) on BIRD.
These comparisons describe answer agreement, including failures, rather than SQL execution success.

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

Operational comparisons used exactly the same context-fit questions as the direct-format analysis:
\num{852} questions from \num{18} Spider databases and \num{303} from two sampled BIRD databases.
All methods retained identical question--model pairs, including \num{372} unsuccessful SQL runs on
Spider and \num{231} on BIRD and their recorded costs. Table~\ref{tab:common-cohort-operational-efficiency}
reports all five shared measures and SQL execution time. SQL tokens and timings accumulated all attempts,
with TTFT summing first-token waits and generation measuring the remaining model time.
Mean SQL retries per run were \num{0.157} (\numrange{0.099}{0.230}) on Spider and
\num{0.314} (\numrange{0.288}{0.337}) on BIRD.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/common_cohort_operational_efficiency}

Text-to-SQL used fewer input tokens than all direct formats
(Figure~\ref{fig:common-cohort-token-usage}).
For CSV, the direct-to-SQL ratio of mean input tokens was \num{7.19} (\numrange{2.38}{13.17})
on Spider and \num{28.91} (\numrange{24.71}{35.63}) on BIRD. Output tokens showed a smaller and
less consistent contrast: Spider CSV produced fewer tokens than SQL in the observed means
(\num{81.2} versus \num{89.7}), but its ratio of \num{0.91} (\numrange{0.65}{1.12}) did not
establish a reliable difference. Only XML's Spider output-token ratio excluded one; on BIRD,
all direct formats produced more output tokens, with paired ratio intervals above one.

Text-to-SQL also had lower mean total latency than every direct format
(Figure~\ref{fig:common-cohort-latency}). On Spider, CSV took \qty{1.255}{\second} against SQL's
\qty{0.466}{\second}, a ratio of \num{2.69} (\numrange{1.06}{4.27}); XML's ratio reached
\num{15.93} (\numrange{2.60}{31.80}). On BIRD, the corresponding ratios were \num{10.43}
(\numrange{9.54}{11.51}) for CSV and \num{66.17} (\numrange{59.32}{74.40}) for XML.
The largest component differences were in TTFT. Generation-only differences were less consistent on
Spider: CSV's direct-to-SQL ratio was \num{1.26} (\numrange{0.73}{1.74}), while only YAML and XML
had intervals entirely above one. All BIRD direct formats had longer generation intervals than SQL.
SQL execution itself contributed only \qty{0.005}{\second} and \qty{0.007}{\second} per run.

Lower mean latency did not imply a faster response for every pair: SQL was faster than CSV in
\qty{58.9}{\percent} of Spider pairs, compared with \qty{98.8}{\percent} on BIRD. The BIRD operational
results describe only two sampled databases. Accuracy and operational results use distinct cohorts,
so they do not establish a joint cost--accuracy trade-off on one matched workload.

\section{Summary}

CSV and TOON had compact payloads, while XML imposed the largest mean token overhead. Direct-context
accuracy varied with format, model, and database size. Text-to-SQL achieved higher answerable-cohort
accuracy than every direct format. Correction recovered executable queries, but failed sequences
consumed most retry time. On the separate context-fit workload, SQL used fewer input tokens and had
lower mean latency, including unsuccessful attempts.
