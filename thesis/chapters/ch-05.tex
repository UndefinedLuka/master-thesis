\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the experiments described in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodolgy} and
\ref{ch:implementation}. Serialization efficiency is presented first, followed by analysis cohorts and
experiment coverage, Direct Context Inference, Text-to-SQL, and a comparison of the two pipelines.
The results cover retrieval accuracy, token use, response latency, and SQL correction performance.
Estimates retain equal weight per run within each dataset. Unless stated otherwise, intervals are pointwise
\qty{95}{\percent} database-bootstrap intervals, following Section~\ref{sec:confidence-intervals};
table cells show these intervals in brackets. BERTScore values are raw (unrescaled).
Paired differences and ratios accompany the main comparisons; an ordering of observed means alone does not
establish a reliable ranking.

\section{Serialization efficiency}
\label{sec:serialization-results}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/serialization_efficiency}

The payload comparison counted only serialized database contents: full Spider databases and the sampled
BIRD databases described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. It used one representative tokenizer from each
of the Qwen~3.5, Ministral~3, and Gemma~4 families, giving \num{558} measurements of \num{31} databases
across six formats and three tokenizer families.

Table~\ref{tab:serialization-efficiency} reports arithmetic means across databases and tokenizer families:
\num{60} observations per Spider format and \num{33} per BIRD format. Relative values are ratios of these
means to CSV, rather than averages of individual ratios. CSV had the lowest mean in both datasets.
TOON added little to its footprint, with ratios of \num{1.04} on Spider and \num{1.03} on BIRD;
XML required \num{3.24} and \num{2.40} times the CSV means. The table retains the intervals and the
intermediate Markdown, YAML, and JSON results. These are total payload footprints for the evaluated
encoder layouts, not isolated syntax-token counts. The means are also sensitive to large databases:
\texttt{wta\_1} contributed \qty{98.76}{\percent} of the pooled Spider CSV total, so the Spider mean
does not represent a typical database size.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/tokenizer_family_comparison.pdf}
  \caption{Median tokenizer-family percentage excess over the smallest token count for each
    database--format pair, separately for Spider and BIRD. Whiskers show pointwise
    \qty{95}{\percent} intervals. Zero median excess does not imply a minimum on every database.}
  \label{fig:tokenizer-family-comparison}
\end{figure}

Tokenizer differences depended on format (Figure~\ref{fig:tokenizer-family-comparison}). Qwen~3.5 had the
lowest median excess for CSV and YAML on both datasets, while Ministral~3 had zero median excess for
Markdown and JSON. For TOON, Gemma~4 had zero median excess on Spider, whereas Qwen~3.5 was closest to the
payload-wise minimum on BIRD. XML favoured Ministral~3 on Spider; Ministral~3 and Gemma~4 both had small
median excess on BIRD. Thus, no tokenizer family was uniformly the most efficient across formats.

At a payload limit of \num{131072} tokens, CSV and TOON fit \num{19} of \num{20} Spider databases for
each tokenizer family; the other formats fit \num{18}. On BIRD, CSV and TOON fit \num{6} of \num{11}
sampled databases, compared with only \num{2} for XML. Markdown fit \num{5} with Qwen~3.5 and \num{6}
with Ministral~3 and Gemma~4. JSON fit \num{3}, \num{4}, and \num{3}, respectively.
Payload fit does not establish complete-request feasibility: other prompt components and output headroom
also require space. The following section therefore turns to the recorded coverage of the actual runs.

\section{Analysis cohorts and experiment coverage}
\label{sec:analysis-cohorts}

Table~\ref{tab:analysis-cohorts} distinguishes the two question-level populations used below. The
answerable cohort contains questions whose stored reference SQL executed without error and returned at
least one row on the experimental database. A row containing zero or NULL still counts as non-empty.
This operational definition does not guarantee question quality or model capability. All accuracy views
use this cohort, retaining context overflows and other terminal pipeline failures with zero scores.
Standalone Text-to-SQL efficiency and correction results use the same questions.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/analysis_cohorts}

The context-fit cohort requires recorded feasibility across all ten retained models and all seven
configurations, with a complete set of \num{70} records per question. The direct runner checked the
rendered fixed system prompt, including data, plus output headroom; it did not check the full
question-bearing chat prompt. Eligibility used that recorded check and inference evidence, together with
all recorded SQL attempts and any backend context errors. It did not depend on reference emptiness,
generated-answer presence, or SQL execution success. Direct and head-to-head efficiency use this identical
question set and include recorded work from unsuccessful runs. These are resource-use measurements on a
shared context-feasible workload, not time per correct answer.

The reference audit found \num{49} empty results among \num{1034} Spider questions and \num{426} among
\num{1534} BIRD questions (\qty{27.77}{\percent}). The latter records concern the materialized sampled
BIRD databases from Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}; they account for the sampling-related empty
references promised there. Empty references were scored in the original scoring pass, but their stored
scores are excluded from answerable-cohort reporting. No reference SQL errors, missing references, or
inconsistent row counts were found. The cohort intersection contained \num{807} Spider and \num{244}
BIRD questions; context-fit also included \num{45} and \num{59} empty-reference questions, respectively.
There were no unknown eligibility cases or missing retained configurations. The BIRD context-fit workload
covered only \texttt{student\_club} and \texttt{toxicology}, so its timing estimates and intervals describe
those two sampled databases.

The Qwen~3.5 27B runs were excluded because the reasoning trace could not be separated reliably from the
final answer. The remaining ten models produced the full-campaign inventory in
Table~\ref{tab:experiment-outcomes}. Its unit is a model--question--method run, and its denominators are
descriptive campaign totals rather than the analysis populations above. Successful completion denotes
pipeline completion, not agreement with the reference answer.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/experiment_outcomes}

Of \num{179760} retained runs, \num{110185} completed and \num{69575} failed. Context overflow accounted
for \num{67536} failures, or \qty{97.1}{\percent} of all failures. The inventory also includes
\num{1984} terminal SQL execution errors and \num{55} failures to extract SQL. These counts describe
the full campaign; the following performance estimates apply the cohorts above.

\section{Direct Context Inference}
\label{sec:direct-results}

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/direct_operational_efficiency}

On the context-fit workload from Section~\ref{sec:analysis-cohorts}, compact formats required fewer
input tokens and had lower mean latency. Table~\ref{tab:direct-operational-efficiency} reports all five
operational measurements. TTFT measures the wait from request submission to the first output token;
generation covers the remaining time to completion, and total latency spans both intervals.
CSV used \num{5366} input tokens per Spider run and \num{31756} per BIRD run,
compared with \num{20186} and \num{97190} for XML. The paired XML-to-CSV ratios were \num{3.76}
(\numrange{2.84}{4.38}) and \num{3.06} (\numrange{2.94}{3.20}), respectively. These are measured prompt
tokens on the context-fit questions, unlike the database-only payload counts in Section~\ref{sec:serialization-results}.

TOON remained close to CSV in input use, with paired ratios of \num{1.07} (\numrange{1.04}{1.12}) on

(Figure~\ref{fig:direct-token-usage-by-format}): the observed means ranged from \numrange{81}{120}
tokens on Spider and \numrange{220}{260} on BIRD. Their ordering differed from input-token use, and
they include both reasoning traces and visible answers.

Mean total latency rose from \qty{1.25}{\second} for CSV to \qty{7.42}{\second} for XML on Spider,
and from \qty{8.02}{\second} to \qty{50.88}{\second} on BIRD
(Figure~\ref{fig:direct-latency-by-format}). The paired XML-to-CSV ratios were \num{5.91}
(\numrange{2.24}{8.31}) and \num{6.34} (\numrange{6.22}{6.47}). Most of the difference occurred in
TTFT: CSV and XML averaged \qty{0.78}{\second} and \qty{6.45}{\second} on Spider, compared with
\qty{6.32}{\second} and \qty{46.87}{\second} on BIRD. Generation after the first token ranged from
\qtyrange{0.48}{0.97}{\second} and \qtyrange{1.70}{4.00}{\second}, respectively. TOON's latency was
closer to CSV, with ratios of \num{1.09} (\numrange{1.04}{1.16}) and \num{1.031}
(\numrange{1.028}{1.033}). The narrow BIRD intervals remain conditional on its two represented databases.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

Accuracy used the answerable cohort, including zero-scored context failures. Each format therefore
summarized \num{9850} Spider and \num{11080} BIRD model--question runs.
Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-format} reports all three retrieval metrics defined in
Section~\ref{sec:reference-answer-metrics}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/direct_accuracy_by_format.pdf}
  \caption{Mean direct-context NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L by format on answerable questions.
    Failed runs retain zero scores. Whiskers show pointwise \qty{95}{\percent} intervals.}
  \label{fig:direct-accuracy-by-format}
\end{figure}

Spider's observed NEM means were close: JSON had the highest value (\num{0.169}), followed by TOON
(\num{0.166}) and CSV (\num{0.163}). JSON exceeded CSV by \num{0.57} percentage points, but the paired
interval (\numrange{-1.21}{2.34}) included zero. CSV had the highest BERTScore (\num{0.812}) and
ROUGE-L (\num{0.336}). Its BERTScore exceeded XML by \num{0.099} (\numrange{0.003}{0.269}), whereas
the TOON-minus-CSV difference was only \num{-0.0004} (\numrange{-0.0020}{0.0011}). Thus, the evidence
separated compact formats from XML more clearly in BERTScore than in NEM.

On BIRD, CSV had the highest means: NEM, BERTScore, and ROUGE-L were \num{0.054}, \num{0.463}, and
\num{0.079}, with TOON close behind. XML had the lowest means (\num{0.026}, \num{0.185}, and
\num{0.042}). Its NEM difference from CSV was \num{-2.75} percentage points
(\numrange{-5.12}{-0.82}), while TOON's difference was \num{-0.31}
(\numrange{-0.79}{0.14}). These estimates include the formats' different context-overflow rates;
they do not isolate answer agreement conditional on inference taking place.

Larger variants generally had higher observed scores, but the size association varied by family and
dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:direct-accuracy-by-model-size}). On Spider, Gemma~4 26B-A4B exceeded E2B by
\num{15.52} NEM percentage points (\numrange{10.19}{21.78}); Qwen~3.5 9B exceeded 0.8B by
\num{14.45} (\numrange{9.60}{20.16}). On BIRD, the corresponding gains were \num{1.58}
(\numrange{-0.02}{3.29}) and \num{4.18} (\numrange{1.48}{7.02}). Ministral~3 14B's gain over 8B was
\num{2.01} points on Spider (\numrange{0.90}{3.21}) but only \num{0.09} on BIRD
(\numrange{-0.72}{1.12}). The latter comparisons do not establish a uniform benefit from nominal
parameter count.

\section{Text-to-SQL}
\label{sec:text-to-sql-results}

All results in this section use the answerable cohort from Section~\ref{sec:analysis-cohorts}:
\num{985} Spider and \num{1108} sampled-BIRD questions, giving \num{9850} and \num{11080} model--question runs.
Failed runs retain their recorded operational costs and zero retrieval scores.

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-operational-efficiency} reports the five shared operational metrics, SQL execution
time, and retries. Input and output tokens, first-token waits, and generation after the first token are
accumulated across all attempts, including unsuccessful correction sequences. Output counts include reasoning
traces; total response latency also includes the recorded aggregate SQL execution time.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_operational_efficiency}

Mean input and output use was \num{811} and \num{107.3} tokens on Spider, compared with \num{1873} and
\num{211.1} on BIRD. Mean total latency was \qty{0.536}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.385}{0.667}{\second}) and
\qty{1.033}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.862}{1.219}{\second}), respectively. Generation after the first token
accounted for most of these totals: \qty{0.441}{\second} on Spider and \qty{0.849}{\second} on BIRD,
compared with cumulative TTFT of \qty{0.084}{\second} and \qty{0.176}{\second}. SQL execution contributed
only \qty{0.010}{\second} and \qty{0.008}{\second}; mean retries were \num{0.173} and \num{0.399}.

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

\subsection{Error correction performance}

\input{../code/analysis/tables/text_to_sql_error_correction}

Under the retry policy in Section~\ref{sec:minimalist-text-to-sql}, execution success increased from
\qty{92.55}{\percent} on the first attempt to \qty{95.23}{\percent} on Spider, and from
\qty{82.23}{\percent} to \qty{89.75}{\percent} on BIRD. Table~\ref{tab:text-to-sql-error-correction}
groups successful runs by the attempt on which SQL first executed successfully and lists terminal
failures; outcome percentages use all eligible runs. Retries recovered \num{264} of \num{734}
initially failed Spider runs, or \qty{35.97}{\percent} (\qtyrange{30.66}{41.49}{\percent}), and \num{833}
of \num{1969} BIRD runs, or \qty{42.31}{\percent} (\qtyrange{38.36}{47.37}{\percent}). Across all
\num{9850} and \num{11080} eligible runs, these recoveries were paired execution-success gains of
\num{2.68} (\numrange{1.66}{3.61}) and \num{7.52} (\numrange{6.36}{8.76}) percentage points.
They measure recovered execution, not recovered answer accuracy.

All initially failed runs entered correction. Mean retries were \num{0.173} per eligible Spider run and
\num{0.399} per eligible BIRD run; among runs entering correction, they were \num{2.32} and \num{2.24}.
Added model-generation time averaged \qty{0.177}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.078}{0.269}{\second}) per eligible
Spider run and \qty{0.455}{\second} (\qtyrange{0.337}{0.587}{\second}) per eligible BIRD run.
Using only runs entering correction as the denominator, the means were \qty{2.38}{\second}
(\qtyrange{1.53}{2.92}{\second}) and \qty{2.56}{\second} (\qtyrange{2.21}{2.96}{\second}).
This retry-generation overhead includes first-token waits. Retry-specific SQL execution time cannot be
isolated from the stored aggregate database time and is excluded from these added-time measurements.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../code/analysis/figures/text_to_sql_error_correction.pdf}
  \caption{Additional Text-to-SQL model-generation time on the answerable cohort, decomposed by terminal
    outcome (left) and retry stage (right). Both panels report seconds per eligible run, using all
    \num{9850} Spider and \num{11080} sampled-BIRD runs as denominators. Time includes first-token waits
    in correction attempts and excludes SQL execution; terminal failures retain their recorded costs.}
  \label{fig:text-to-sql-error-correction}
\end{figure}

The first retry supplied most recoveries: \num{238} on Spider and \num{680} on BIRD. Later stages still
accounted for substantial generation time (Figure~\ref{fig:text-to-sql-error-correction}). The three stages
added \qty{0.063}{\second}, \qty{0.057}{\second}, and \qty{0.057}{\second} per eligible Spider run,
and \qty{0.174}{\second}, \qty{0.143}{\second}, and \qty{0.138}{\second} per eligible BIRD run.
Terminal failures accounted for \qty{95.3}{\percent} of all retry-generation time on Spider and
\qty{88.3}{\percent} on BIRD. The \num{470} and \num{1136} failed sequences each used all three retries,
with mean added generation time of \qty{3.54}{\second} and \qty{3.92}{\second} per failed run.
Thus, most recorded correction cost belonged to sequences that never produced executable SQL.

\section{Comparison of the two pipelines}
\label{sec:common-cohort-comparison}

\subsection{Retrieval accuracy}

The accuracy comparison used all \num{985} answerable Spider questions and \num{1108} answerable BIRD
questions, paired across the ten retained models and all six direct formats. Context overflows and other
terminal failures remained in the comparison with zero scores. Text-to-SQL had higher mean NEM,
BERTScore, and ROUGE-L than every direct format on both datasets; all paired difference intervals
excluded zero (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline-retrieval-comparison}).

On Spider, Text-to-SQL reached NEM \num{0.634}, compared with \num{0.169} for JSON, the highest direct
mean. The paired JSON-minus-SQL difference was \num{-0.465} (\numrange{-0.507}{-0.422}). On sampled
BIRD, Text-to-SQL reached \num{0.501}, compared with CSV's \num{0.054}, a difference of
\num{-0.447} (\numrange{-0.524}{-0.380}). CSV had the highest direct BERTScore and ROUGE-L means
on both datasets, but its paired differences from SQL were still \num{-0.104}
(\numrange{-0.229}{-0.038}) and \num{-0.403} (\numrange{-0.450}{-0.359}) on Spider, and
\num{-0.385} (\numrange{-0.661}{-0.137}) and \num{-0.438} (\numrange{-0.508}{-0.367}) on BIRD.
These comparisons describe answer agreement, including failures, rather than SQL execution success.

\subsection{Operational efficiency}

Operational comparisons used exactly the same context-fit questions as the direct-format analysis:
\num{852} questions from \num{18} Spider databases and \num{303} from two sampled BIRD databases.
All methods retained identical question--model pairs, including \num{372} unsuccessful SQL runs on
Spider and \num{231} on BIRD and their recorded costs. Table~\ref{tab:common-cohort-operational-efficiency}
reports all five shared measures and SQL execution time. SQL tokens and timings accumulated all attempts,
with TTFT summing first-token waits and generation measuring the remaining model time.
Mean SQL retries per run were \num{0.157} (\numrange{0.099}{0.230}) on Spider and
\num{0.314} (\numrange{0.288}{0.337}) on BIRD.

\input{../code/analysis/tables/common_cohort_operational_efficiency}

Text-to-SQL used fewer input tokens than all direct formats
(Figure~\ref{fig:common-cohort-token-usage}).
For CSV, the direct-to-SQL ratio of mean input tokens was \num{7.19} (\numrange{2.38}{13.17})
on Spider and \num{28.91} (\numrange{24.71}{35.63}) on BIRD. Output tokens showed a smaller and
less consistent contrast: Spider CSV produced fewer tokens than SQL in the observed means
(\num{81.2} versus \num{89.7}), but its ratio of \num{0.91} (\numrange{0.65}{1.12}) did not
establish a reliable difference. Only XML's Spider output-token ratio excluded one; on BIRD,
all direct formats produced more output tokens, with paired ratio intervals above one.

Text-to-SQL also had lower mean total latency than every direct format
(Figure~\ref{fig:common-cohort-latency}). On Spider, CSV took \qty{1.255}{\second} against SQL's
\qty{0.466}{\second}, a ratio of \num{2.69} (\numrange{1.06}{4.27}); XML's ratio reached
\num{15.93} (\numrange{2.60}{31.80}). On BIRD, the corresponding ratios were \num{10.43}
(\numrange{9.54}{11.51}) for CSV and \num{66.17} (\numrange{59.32}{74.40}) for XML.
The largest component differences were in TTFT. Generation-only differences were less consistent on
Spider: CSV's direct-to-SQL ratio was \num{1.26} (\numrange{0.73}{1.74}), while only YAML and XML
had intervals entirely above one. All BIRD direct formats had longer generation intervals than SQL.
SQL execution itself contributed only \qty{0.005}{\second} and \qty{0.007}{\second} per run.

Lower mean latency did not imply a faster response for every pair: SQL was faster than CSV in
\qty{58.9}{\percent} of Spider pairs, compared with \qty{98.8}{\percent} on BIRD. The BIRD operational
results describe only two sampled databases. Accuracy and operational results use distinct cohorts,
so they do not establish a joint cost--accuracy trade-off on one matched workload.

\section{Summary}

CSV and TOON had compact payloads, while XML imposed the largest mean token overhead. Direct-context
accuracy varied with format, model, and database size. Text-to-SQL achieved higher answerable-cohort
accuracy than every direct format. Correction recovered executable queries, but failed sequences
consumed most retry time. On the separate context-fit workload, SQL used fewer input tokens and had
lower mean latency, including unsuccessful attempts.
